Water Insecurity in the United States

Visualizing where households lack indoor plumbing and how it relates to other county characteristics

Author

Team Water - Jesus Castillo, Deepak Ramesh, Jonathan Le Roux 

library(tidyverse)
library(sf)

Project goal

We will map the share of U.S. households that lack complete indoor plumbing, county by county, and show how it changed between 2022 and 2023. We will then build a 3D map in which the color shows the plumbing rate and the height shows population density. Finally, we will explore how the rate relates to other county characteristics.

Goals and motivation

Having a working kitchen and bathroom at home is something most people take for granted, but a small share of U.S. households do not have it. Averages for the whole country or for a state hide where these households are concentrated. A map lets readers see local patterns, and the 3D view lets them see whether high rates occur in crowded places or in sparsely populated ones. Because the raw percentages are small and very uneven, the way we design the color scale and the height scale will matter as much as the data.

We will look at all counties in the dataset, and then zoom in on regions with the highest rates. We expect the pattern to be uneven across the country, but we will let the maps show us where.

Dataset

Where the data comes from: The data comes from the U.S. Census Bureau’s American Community Survey (ACS) and was published by TidyTuesday on 2025-01-28 (source). It accompanies a U.S. Geological Survey (USGS) blog post on mapping water insecurity with the tidycensus package.

Why we chose it: It is real data that covers the whole country and includes county boundaries, so it is a natural fit for maps. It has two years, so we can compare change over time, and it has a county ID that lets us merge in other Census data.

Size of the data: The dataset has 1,702 rows and 7 columns. Each row is one county in one year, and it covers 854 counties across 52 states and territories. The data appears to include only larger counties, since the 2023 file has far fewer than the more than 3,000 counties in the U.S. We will check this against the ACS documentation and describe it clearly in the write-up, because small rural counties may be exactly where the highest rates are.

Missing values and geometry: Some counties have NA for the plumbing counts (3 rows have a missing percent), and we will show them in a separate color on the maps instead of dropping them. The geometry column is saved as text, a flat list of x coordinates followed by y coordinates. We will rebuild it into sf polygons before mapping.

Variable Type Description
geoid character Census county ID (FIPS code)
name character County and state name
year character ACS year (2022 or 2023)
geometry text County boundary, saved as text
total_pop number Total population
plumbing number Households lacking complete plumbing
percent_lacking_plumbing number Percent of households lacking complete plumbing

Analysis plan

A plan for the project: We will first rebuild the county polygons and check the data for missing values. We will then calculate each county’s area from the polygons (they are already in an equal-area projection, EPSG:5070) and use it to calculate population density. Next, we will map the plumbing rate for each year and the change between years, and finally combine rate and density in a 3D map. We will also merge in other county variables to see how the rate relates to them.

Variables involved: geoid, name, year, geometry, total_pop, plumbing and percent_lacking_plumbing.

Variables to be created: sf polygons rebuilt from the text geometry, a state column taken from name, county area, population density (people per square kilometer), the change in percent lacking plumbing from 2022 to 2023, and median household income and poverty rate from the Census Bureau.

External data to be merged in: median household income and poverty rate for each county, pulled with tidycensus from the same ACS and joined by geoid. We may also add a rural-urban classification if we can find one that matches the county IDs.

Measure Rule What it tells us
Plumbing rate percent_lacking_plumbing for each county and year Where households lack complete plumbing
Change 2023 rate minus 2022 rate for each county Where the rate got better or worse
Population density total_pop divided by county area in km² Whether high rates occur in dense or sparse counties
Related variables Spearman correlation between the rate and income, poverty and density How the rate moves with other county characteristics

We will use Spearman correlations because the rates are small and heavily right-skewed. For the maps, we will use a transformed color scale (for example, a square-root or log scale) so that counties with moderate rates are not all shown in the same color as counties with zero. Counties that appear in only one year will be left out of the change map and counted in the write-up.

Visualization plan

We will make three main figures and one summary table. Together they show where the rates are high, how they changed, and how they relate to density and other variables.

Main chart: 3D map of rate and density:

Each county is a column on a map of the U.S. The color shows the percent of households lacking plumbing, and the height shows population density. Readers can rotate the view and compare tall, dense counties with flat, sparse ones. Because density is very uneven (a few counties are far denser than the rest), we will scale the height carefully and say so in the caption. We will make a static version for the write-up and an interactive or rotating version for the presentation.

Supporting chart: Maps over time:

Side-by-side maps for 2022 and 2023 share the same color scale, so they can be compared directly. A third map shows the change in percentage points. If time allows, we will turn these into an interactive map or an animation that moves between the two years.

Supporting chart: Relationships:

Scatterplots of the plumbing rate against income, poverty and population density, using a log scale where needed. Each point is a county, and the points are colored by region so that readers can see whether patterns differ across the country.

Summary table:

A table in the write-up will list the ten counties with the highest rates in each year, with their population and density, so that readers can see how many people are affected.

Design choices

  • Every figure will have a clear title, axis labels with units, and a legend that explains the colors.

  • We will use a color-blind-friendly sequential palette for the rates and a diverging palette for the change.

  • Counties with missing data will have their own color, so they are not mistaken for a rate of zero.

  • All figures will be created from R scripts in the scripts/ folder, so anyone can run the project and get the same results.

What we will teach ourselves:

For our independent-learning component, we will learn three things that were not covered in class. First, we will use sf to rebuild and work with the county boundaries and calculate area. Second, we will use rayshader to turn a ggplot2 map into an interactive 3D visualization. Third, we will use tidycensus to download Census data directly into R using the Census API. We will check with our instructor that this qualifies as new material.
